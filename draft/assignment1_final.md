## THE MARKET OFFERING

Cozy Yellow Label Black Tea Bags (Trà Đen Nhãn Vàng Cozy) is a packaged black tea product manufactured by Công ty Thế Hệ Mới (Future Generation Co., Ltd.), a Vietnamese tea enterprise founded in 1996 and established the Cozy consumer brand in 2003. The product analysed is the 50-gram retail format, containing 25 individually wrapped tea bags of 2 grams each. At Lotte Mart Đà Nẵng (Unit 1F-09, 06 Nại Nam Street), the product was observed at 34,300 Vietnamese Đồng (VND), which places it in a practical value segment for everyday tea buyers. That positioning matters because tea in this category is not bought as a luxury item; it is usually chosen as a routine household staple, so familiarity and convenience are central to the offering.

The market offering provides three layers of benefit. Functionally, Cozy Yellow Label delivers 100 percent pure black tea in a convenient tea-bag format that supports easy brewing at home and in beverage preparation settings (Cozy, 2023). The product is also marketed for fruit tea and milk tea preparation, which broadens its use beyond household consumption to small cafés and food service operations (Cozy, 2023). Socially, the brand signals domestic origin and traceability through plantation sourcing across Vietnamese tea-growing regions and certifications such as ISO 22000, HACCP, GMP, and Halal. Psychologically, the brand promises comfort and cultural familiarity through the idea of "những phút giây thư giãn" (moments of relaxation) while supporting a Vietnamese producer that invests in quality control and export capability (Cozy, 2023). Vietnamese research shows that traceability, certification, and local-origin signalling can raise trust and willingness to pay for food and tea products, so these cues are commercially meaningful rather than decorative (Dang Duc Huy et al., 2019; Truong et al., 2021; Duc Tran et al., 2019; Nguyen Thuy Trang et al., 2024; Minh Hue Nguyen et al., 2025). Taken together, those benefits make the product feel reliable rather than experimental, which is important in a low-involvement grocery category.

## THE MARKETING MIX

The marketing mix framework, expressed as Product, Price, Place, and Promotion, helps explain how Cozy aligns its offer with customer needs (Armstrong et al., 2021). For Cozy Yellow Label, the key issue is not only product quality, but also how the product is priced, placed, and promoted for a value-conscious consumer. In practice, the brand relies on a clear combination of domestic identity, visible quality cues, and accessible retail channels. That matters in Vietnam because tea consumers do not evaluate an item in isolation; they also respond to trust in the label, the retailer, the origin story, and whether the product looks safe enough to repurchase. Studies on Vietnamese consumers show that traceability and certification can influence willingness to pay, while OCOP-style local products gain value when the consumer sees both private quality signals and public assurance (Dang Duc Huy et al., 2019; Truong et al., 2021; Duc Tran et al., 2022; Nguyen Thuy Trang et al., 2024; Minh Hue Nguyen et al., 2025).

### Product

The core product is black tea. The actual product is the 25-bag retail box, while the augmented product is the set of quality signals around it: branded packaging, plantation sourcing, and food-safety certification. These cues matter because they reduce perceived risk in a category that is often bought quickly and with low deliberation. In this case, Cozy uses visible quality claims to support a mid-market position rather than a premium one. The box is therefore doing more than holding tea bags; it is carrying the brand story that helps the consumer decide quickly at shelf level.

### Place

Cozy uses a hybrid distribution strategy across modern retail, online marketplaces, and the brand website. The Lotte Mart setting matters because it places the product in a structured, comparison-friendly environment where shoppers can compare brands, prices, and pack sizes side by side. The same product also benefits from e-commerce discoverability and direct brand authority through the Cozy website, so the consumer can encounter the brand both physically and digitally without changing the basic product promise. Research on tea e-commerce and food-label valuation suggests that information reliability, convenience, and trust help turn online interest into actual purchase behaviour, which makes the multi-channel approach commercially sensible rather than redundant (K. Xie et al., 2023; Cam Anh Thi Pham et al., 2025; Sihang Rao et al., 2023).

### Promotion

Promotion relies on shelf visibility, packaging, and digital presence. For a low-involvement grocery item such as tea, these cues are usually more important than long-form persuasion. The marketing logic is simple: make the product easy to notice, easy to trust, and easy to repurchase. That is why the pack design, certification icons, and retail placement matter as much as any promotional message.

## MAPPING MY DECISION-MAKING

Consumer decision-making for tea can be explained through five stages: need recognition, information search, evaluation, purchase decision, and post-purchase behaviour (Armstrong et al., 2021). This framework is useful because the purchase is usually routine, but it still involves small moments of comparison and judgement.

**Stage 1: Need Recognition.** I notice that I am running low on tea, or I want a convenient option for daily drinking and beverage preparation. The need is practical rather than emotional, but it is still shaped by habit and comfort.

**Stage 2: Information Search.** At Lotte Mart Đà Nẵng, I recall Cozy as a familiar Vietnamese brand and glance at competing products. In Vietnam, trust in certification and food-safety cues can shape purchase intentions for certified products (Ngo et al., 2021). I am not doing a deep search; instead, I am checking whether the product still matches my prior experience and whether the shelf presentation looks trustworthy. Vietnamese evidence on OCOP, food labelling, and certification also suggests that consumers respond differently depending on their trust in the information system, not just the product itself (Nguyen Thuy Trang et al., 2024; Truong et al., 2021; Duc Tran et al., 2022).

**Stage 3: Evaluation of Alternatives.** I compare the product against other teas using price, familiarity, package size, and visible quality signals. The evaluation is fairly quick, but those criteria matter because they reduce uncertainty and make it easier to justify the choice. Tea-specific studies in Vietnam also show that traceability, certification, and origin cues influence choice and willingness to pay, which makes this kind of shelf-level comparison very plausible (Dang Duc Huy et al., 2019; Duc Tran et al., 2019; Minh Hue Nguyen et al., 2025).

**Stage 4: Purchase Decision.** Because this is a repeat-purchase category, habit and context matter more than extended deliberation (Danner et al., 2008; Ji et al., 2007; Neale et al., 2011). If the shelf is stocked and the price still feels reasonable, the decision becomes almost automatic.

**Stage 5: Post-Purchase Behaviour.** If the taste, convenience, and value are satisfactory, I am more likely to repurchase the same box next time. If the flavour feels weak or the package seems inconvenient, that would create a small but real reason to switch.

## MY CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE

Customer experience (CX) is shaped by the full set of interactions a consumer has with a product and retail environment (Gahler et al., 2023). For Cozy Yellow Label, the strongest part of the experience is sensory coherence. The product is not memorable because it is flashy; it is memorable because the shelf, pack, and store environment fit together cleanly.

Visually, the yellow-gold box stands out against cooler competitor packaging and is easy to locate. Auditorily, the store creates a calm but active shopping atmosphere. Tactually, the box feels lightweight and convenient to carry. Olfactorily, the tea aisle stays neutral rather than overpowering. Gustatively, the product promise is a full-bodied black-tea taste suited to mixing or straight brewing. Together, these cues lower decision effort and make the purchase feel straightforward rather than risky. The overall effect is not luxury; it is confidence, convenience, and a sense of familiarity.

That experience is consistent with sensory-marketing research showing that coordinated sensory cues can improve customer response in retail settings (Spence et al., 2014; Doucé et al., 2020; Duong et al., 2022). It also explains why the product feels easy to choose and easy to imagine repurchasing. For a routine tea purchase, that ease is a meaningful part of customer experience. Tea-authentication studies also suggest that product information, trust, and sensory descriptors shape whether consumers believe a tea is authentic and worth repurchasing (Meiyi Chen et al., 2023; Tsung-Chen Su et al., 2021; Xiaoling Liu et al., 2015).
