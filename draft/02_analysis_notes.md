## TASK 1 — Consumer Decision-Making Process

### Stage 1: Need Recognition
- (a) Academic definition: Need recognition occurs when a consumer perceives a gap between their current state and desired state, often triggered by an internal cue or an external stimulus that activates a purchase need (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (b) Applied example: I realise I need a convenient tea product when I want to make trà chanh or milk tea at home, or when I want something light to help me feel refreshed and concentrate while studying. Because I am already shopping at Lotte Mart Đà Nẵng, I notice that I am running low on tea bags and that Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags are a practical, familiar option for my weekly shopping trip.
- (c) Assignment-ready sentence: I recognise the need for Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags when I want an affordable and convenient way to prepare tea drinks at home, which reflects the first stage of the consumer decision-making process (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (d) Visual icon suggestion: A light bulb icon from Icons8 or Flaticon fits because it symbolises the moment the consumer becomes aware of a need.

### Stage 2: Information Search
- (a) Academic definition: After need recognition, consumers seek information to reduce uncertainty by searching internal memory and external sources such as friends, stores, advertising, packaging, and online reviews (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (b) Applied example: I remember that Cozy is a familiar tea brand and I scan the shelf at Lotte Mart for the 25-bag box, price label, and competing brands such as Lipton, Phúc Long, Vinatea, Dilmah, and Twinings. I may also compare the box design, the number of bags, and whether the price at Lotte Mart makes Cozy look like a good value for a student budget.
- (c) Assignment-ready sentence: I search for information on Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags by comparing the shelf price, pack size, and brand familiarity with competing tea brands before deciding whether to buy (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (d) Visual icon suggestion: A magnifying glass icon from Icons8 or Flaticon fits because it represents the consumer actively looking for information.

### Stage 3: Evaluation of Alternatives
- (a) Academic definition: In the evaluation stage, consumers compare alternative brands or products using the attributes that matter most to them, such as price, quality, taste, convenience, and perceived risk (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (b) Applied example: At the tea aisle in Lotte Mart, I compare Cozy with other brands on affordability, Vietnamese familiarity, box size, expected tea strength, and suitability for making lemon tea or milk tea. Because I am a student, I would probably prefer a product that looks trustworthy, is easy to use at home, and does not feel unnecessarily premium for an everyday drink.
- (c) Assignment-ready sentence: I evaluate Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags by comparing price, flavour strength, and convenience with competing tea brands, and I pay close attention to whether the product suits my routine home-drink needs (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (d) Visual icon suggestion: A balance scales icon from Flaticon or a check-list emoji fits because it shows comparison across alternatives.

### Stage 4: Purchase Decision
- (a) Academic definition: The purchase decision stage is when the consumer forms an intention and chooses whether, when, where, and what to buy, although the final outcome can still be altered by other people or unexpected situational factors (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (b) Applied example: If Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags are in stock and the price at Lotte Mart is acceptable, I would most likely place the box in my basket and buy it. This is a repeat purchase rather than a trial purchase because Cozy is a widely available, affordable mass-market product that already fits my tea-making routine and my budget.
- (c) Assignment-ready sentence: I would treat Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags as a repeat purchase because it is an affordable mass-market product that already fits my routine, so my final decision depends mainly on shelf availability and price at Lotte Mart (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (d) Visual icon suggestion: A shopping cart icon from Icons8 or Flaticon fits because it represents the final act of selecting the product for purchase.
- Purchase type: repeat purchase.
- Disruptor 1, attitudes of others: If I am shopping with a roommate or family member, they may suggest Lipton, Phúc Long, or a cheaper store-brand tea instead of Cozy, especially if they care more about taste preference or brand prestige than convenience.
- Disruptor 2, unexpected situational factors: I may decide not to buy if the Cozy box is out of stock, if another tea is on promotion at the same shelf, or if I am rushed and do not want to spend extra time comparing labels in the aisle.

### Stage 5: Post-Purchase Behaviour
- (a) Academic definition: After purchase, consumers evaluate whether the product met expectations, and they may experience satisfaction, dissatisfaction, or cognitive dissonance that influences future behaviour and repeat purchase intentions (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (b) Applied example: After I make tea at home, I judge whether Cozy produces a strong enough flavour for trà chanh or milk tea and whether the box offers good value for the money I spent at Lotte Mart. If the tea tastes consistent and convenient to use, I am likely to buy it again on my next grocery trip.
- (c) Assignment-ready sentence: After buying Cozy Yellow Label Tea Bags, I evaluate whether the flavour, convenience, and value meet my expectations, which will shape whether I buy the product again in future (Armstrong et al., 2021).
- (d) Visual icon suggestion: A star, thumbs-up, or smiling-face icon fits because it symbolises satisfaction and post-purchase evaluation.
