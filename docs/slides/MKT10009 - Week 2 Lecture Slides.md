Consumers are becoming ‘prosumers’
• Consumers are no longer merely passive, but rather becoming more active ‘prosumers’.
• “Individuals who consume and produce value, either for self-consumption or
consumption by others, and can receive implicit or explicit incentives from organizations
involved in the exchange” (Lang et al. 2021, p. 178)
• Prosumers consume value (e.g. through using products) but then can also produce it for
others and be incentivised for doing so:
• Posting reviews and ratings
• Creating unboxing videos, tutorials, or TikTok content
• Sharing brand experiences on social media
• Can advocate for or against brands
• Incentivised through recognition, engagement, potentially monetary (sponsorship)
Lang, B., Dolan, R., Kemper, J., & Northey, G. (2021). Prosumers in times of crisis: definition, archetypes and implications.Journal of Service Management,32(2), 176-189. https://doi.org/10.1108/JOSM-05-2020-0155

Consumer buyer behaviour
Consumer buyer behaviour refers to the “buying behaviour of final consumers –
individuals and households – that buy goods and services for personal consumption”
(Armstrong et al., 2021, p. 136).
• Learning about the ‘whys’ of consumer buying behaviour is not easy. Often,
consumers themselves do not know exactly what influences their purchases.
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Model of Buyer Behaviour
• The Model of Buyer Behaviour visualises how marketing and other environmental factors are
subjectively processed and responded to by the consumer
• Environmental factors are interpreted within the consumer’s ‘black box’ (buyer characteristics and their
decision process)
• Consumers enact a response (buying attitudes and preferences, buying behaviour, relationship
formation with brands)

Social factors
Social factors include the consumer’s small groups, family, social role and social status.
Groups and social networks influence a person’s behaviour:
• Reference groups: Groups whose influence plays a direct or indirect part in consumer
buying behaviour. They serve as a point of comparison or ‘reference’ in forming a
consumer’s attitudes or behaviour.
• Membership groups: A reference group to which an individual belongs and are
directly influenced by.
• Aspirational groups: A reference group with whom a person desires to be associated.
• Opinion leaders: Person within a reference group who exerts influence over others,
owing to their skills, knowledge, personality, popularity, or other characteristics.
• Online social networks: Online social communities (social networking sites, online
communities etc) where people socialize or exchange information and opinions.
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Social factors
Family
Family members can strongly influence buyer behaviour (e.g.
parents, children).
Roles and status
People usually choose products appropriate to their roles and
status. E.g. A mother works as a brand manager:
• Her parental role requires her to purchase specific food, medication,
clothing etc for her children, but her professional role requires her to
purchase corporate clothing and jewelry to project professionalism and her
status in the company
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Personal factors
A buyer’s decisions are also influenced by personal characteristics,
such as their age and life-cycle stage, occupation, economic
situation, lifestyle, personality, and self concept.
Age and life-cycle stage
Our purchase behaviour is influenced by our age.
As we move through life stages (e.g. growing up, living out of
home, getting married, having children, purchasing a home,
retiring… etc.) our purchasing behaviour changes.
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Personal factors
Occupation
A person’s occupation affects the products they buy.
• E.g. a construction worker needs rugged work clothes and steel-capped
boots, whereas an office worker might want noise cancelling
headphones for their commute
Economic situation
A person’s economic situation will affect their store and product choices.
Will influence the amount of discretionary income they have (save vs
spend) and how they prioritise their spending
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Personal factors
Lifestyle
Lifestyle is a person’s pattern of living as expressed in their
activities, interests and opinions. It’s the profile of a person’s
pattern of acting and interacting in the world.
• Activities: Hobbies, shopping, sports, social events
• Interests: Food, fashion, family, recreation
• Opinions: About themselves, social issues, business, products
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Personal factors
Personality
Personality is the unique psychological characteristics that
distinguish a person or group.
Big five personality traits (OCEAN): Openness,
Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and
Neuroticism.
Badgaiyan and Verma (2014) find that:
• Extraverted consumers are more likely to engage in impulsive buying
behaviour
• Conscientious consumers are less likely to engage in impulsive
buying behaviour
We buy which reflects our self-image – “we are what we have”.

Psychological factors
A person’s buying choices are influenced by four main psychological factors:
• motivation
• perception
• learning
• beliefs and attitudes

Psychological factors
Motivation
Motive (drive) is a need that is sufficiently pressing to direct the person to
seek satisfaction. Many different theories which explain why humans are
motivated to fulfil needs.
A (very) basic categorisation of needs we’re motivated to satisfy:
• Innate Needs: Physiological (or biogenic) needs that are considered
primary needs or motives. They are required to sustain life. e.g. a person
is motivated to seek food, shelter, sleep etc.
• Acquired needs: Generally psychological (or psychogenic) needs that
are considered secondary needs or motives. We have acquired or
learned them in response to our environment and culture. e.g. a person
is motivated to seek self esteem, prestige, affection etc.
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.
Solomon, M., Russell-Bennett, R., & Previte, J. (2013). Consumer Behaviour: Buying, Having, Being (3rd ed.). Pearson Education, Australia.

Psychological factors
Perception
Perception is the process by which people select, organise and
interpret information to form a meaningful picture of the world.
Inputs (sensory stimuli) picked up by our five senses (sensory
receptors) are the raw data that begin the perceptual process.
To cope with the overwhelming amount of stimuli, consumers engage
in distinct perceptual processes to cope:
• Selective attention: screening out most of the information they’re
exposed to and being selective with their attention
• Selective distortion: interpret information in a way that supports
what they already believe.
• Selective retention: remember information that supports existing
attitudes and beliefs.
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Psychological factors
Learning
Learning refers to the changes in an individual’s behaviour arising from
experience. It occurs through the interplay of drives, stimuli, cues,
responses and reinforcement.
• A drive is a strong internal stimulus that calls for action directed
towards a stimulus object.
• Cues are minor stimuli that determine when, where and how the
person responds. Consumers are conditioned by surrounding cues.
• Reinforcement occurs through receiving desired outcome or having
positive experience
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Psychological factors
Beliefs and attitudes
A belief is a descriptive thought that a person has about something.
May be based on real knowledge, opinion or faith, and may or may not
be emotionally charged.
Attitude refers to a person’s consistently favourable or unfavourable
evaluations, feelings and tendencies towards an object or idea.
Attitudes comprise three parts:
• Cognition: thoughts and beliefs towards the object
• Affect: feelings towards the object
• Conation: an intention to behave in a certain way towards the object
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

The Consumer Decision Process
• The Consumer (or Buyer) Decision Process refers to the series of steps a
consumer goes through when deciding whether to purchase a product or
service.
• Marketers need to understand these steps to effectively influence consumer
decision making at specific stages
Armstrong G., Denize S., Volkov M., Adam S., Kotler P., Ang S., Love A., Doherty S. & van Esch P. (2021).Principles of Marketing (8thed.). Pearson Australia.

Need recognition
The buyer recognises a problem or need. It can be
triggered by internal stimuli (e.g. hunger, thirst) or
external stimuli (e.g. an advertisement).
Marketers should aim to understand what kinds of needs
or problems arise for consumers and why these exist.

Information search
The buyer seeks out information about products or services with the
potential to satisfy the need. This informs which products we consider
within our purchase decision.
• Personal sources: family, friends, neighbours, acquaintances
• Commercial sources: advertising, salespeople, websites, packaging,
displays
• Public sources: mass media, consumer reviews, social media, internet
searches
• Experiential sources: handling, examining, trialling/using the product
• Marketers should understand this stage to gain insights into the
sources they seek and the risks they perceive

Evaluation of alternatives
The consumer processes information to arrive at brand choices.
Consumers tend to use two types of information in their evaluation:
• A ‘list’ of brands/stores from which they plan to make their
selection (the ‘consideration set’)
• The criteria they will use to evaluate each alternative (i.e. product
attributes)
• E.g. for a car: price, style, performance, safety
• Marketers should study buyers to find out how they actually
evaluate brand alternatives so that they can take steps to assist
buyers in their decision making.

Purchase decision
The consumer forms a purchase intention and ultimately makes the purchase.
• Trial purchase: When a consumer purchases a product (or brand) for the first time and
buys a smaller quantity than usual.
• Repeat purchase: Consumers purchase the product again because it has been found by
trial to be satisfactory or better than other alternatives.
• Long-term commitment purchase: A buying decision where the consumer moves
directly from evaluating options to making a purchase without the opportunity for a trial,
and the product is intended for extended use over time.
Two factors can come between the purchase intention (intending to purchase) and
purchase decision (committing to purchase):
• Attitudes of others: what others think, which may disrupt your intentions
• Unexpected situational factors: unexpected events which may disrupt your intentions

Post-purchase behaviour
Following the purchase, the consumer will engage in post-purchase behaviour depending
on their level of satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the purchase.
• Customer expectations less than perceived performance = consumer is dissatisfied
• Post-purchase behaviour: bad word-of-mouth (friends, family, social media), complaints
to the company, future product or brand avoidance
• Perceived performance equals customer expectations = consumer is satisfied
• Perceived performance greater than customer expectations = consumer is delighted
• Post-purchase behaviour: positive word-of-mouth, pay less attention to competing
brands and advertising, future product or brand purchases
• Delighted consumers are more loyal, so marketers should go beyond merely satisfying
them
